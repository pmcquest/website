Research

My research sits at the intersection of comparative political economy and peace studies, focusing on conflict legacies, policy design, and extractive enterprises in Latin America. I am motivated to understand conflict transformation processes, the protection of human and environmental rights, and the roots of ethical political economy.

Dissertation: Mining for Peace

Conflict Settlements and Extractivism in Colombia and Peru

My dissertation examines the political and institutional dynamics of peacebuilding interventions in resource-rich environments, using Colombia as a primary case to unpack how the type of conflict settlement shapes subnational governance of extractive industries over time — and Peru as a contrasting case where no comprehensive peace process took place. Across both cases, I am developing a typology of extractive governance in conflict-affected countries, contributing to research on the political economy of developing extractive economies.

In the Colombia chapters, I use comparative historical analysis, interviews with negotiators, and archival research to interrogate the revenue-based motivations of government negotiators and insurgent or paramilitary groups in designing partial peace agreements in strategic resource-rich regions, and to ask how the localized presence of multi-cultural ethnic and campesino groups in resource-rich zones may shape accountability dynamics resembling third-party monitoring. I also examine whether this dynamic extends beyond partial agreements into the design of more comprehensive frameworks, including the 2016 agreement with the FARC-EP — tracing the decision-making behind incorporating multi-cultural populations as protagonists in truth and reconciliation processes, as arbiters of investment projects, and as guardians of the natural environments where resources are most contested.

The Peru chapter interrogates this “peace learning” hypothesis in a setting where the absence of partial or comprehensive peace processes between the government and insurgent groups may produce markedly different governance dynamics. Resource-rich areas there are highly contested by private speculators and local communities; I ask whether the absence of formal elite pacts — from which repertoires of accountability could otherwise be forged — helps explain more costly collective action, and in some cases, governance capture by criminal enterprises.

Broader Research Agenda

Measuring subnational democracy. In a co-authored project, I apply comparative area expertise in Colombia to introduce a new methodology for measuring subnational levels of democracy, combining empirical data with Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem) expert survey responses. I demonstrate this method in my dissertation by comparing democracy levels across mining sites in Colombia and Peru.

Civil society and environmental politics in the Peruvian Amazon. Field research in Madre de Dios motivated a line of work on the role of the Catholic Church in organizing civic action against illegal alluvial mining and shaping environmental policy — examining how Church leaders and lay people coordinate with Indigenous groups and other religious actors to influence environmental governance.

Networks. I am affiliated with the Forests and Livelihoods: Assessment, Research, and Engagement (FLARE) network, an interdisciplinary community of scholars working on environmental sustainability, and my research has been supported by the Kellogg Institute for International Studies and the Laudato Si Institute, among others.
